# 07 · Dashboards

The final stage of the project is a set of Metabase dashboards built on the gold tables in Redshift. They bring the transformed data together to answer the main business questions: where revenue comes from, which products perform best, what drives refunds, and how purchasing behaviour differs across customer groups and sales channels.

This page walks through the dashboard structure, the data behind each section, and a few things to keep in mind when interpreting the results.

For the tables and transformations that support the dashboards, see [05 · Data model](05-data-model.md).

---

## 1. How it is set up

Metabase sits at the reporting end of the pipeline. It connects directly to the gold layer in Redshift, where the data has already been cleaned, transformed and organised for analysis.

A few details about the setup:

- **Metabase runs locally in Docker.** The configuration lives in `bi/docker-compose.yml`, and the application is available at [http://localhost:3000](http://localhost:3000).
- **It connects to Redshift through `bi_reader`.** This is a dedicated read-only user with access to the `marts` schema and nothing else. See [03 · Infrastructure](03-infrastructure.md) for the access configuration.
- **Each chart is built from a gold table.** The dashboard queries the prepared models in `marts` rather than accessing raw or staging data. Calculations that belong in the data model are handled there, keeping the dashboard focused on presenting the results.
- **The data updates with the pipeline.** Whenever a new workbook is processed, the gold tables are rebuilt. Reloading the dashboard then displays the latest figures without requiring changes to the questions or charts.

The dashboards are organised into three sections:

| Section | Business question |
|---|---|
| 1 · Overview | How much revenue does the business generate, and where and when does it come from? |
| 2 · Products & Refunds | Which products and categories perform best, and what does refund activity look like? |
| 3 · Customers & Commercial | Who is buying, which channels and payment methods do they use, and how do discounts affect purchasing behaviour? |

## 2. Section 1: Overview

The Overview section provides a high-level picture of sales activity. It brings together headline revenue figures, regional comparisons and monthly trends.

| Card | What it shows | Source table |
|---|---|---|
| Total Revenue (USD) | Headline revenue, including tax | `revenue_by_month_region` |
| Total Transactions | Number of transactions | `revenue_by_month_region` |
| Revenue by Month and Region | Monthly revenue trend, with a separate line for each region | `revenue_by_month_region` |
| Revenue by Region | Total revenue generated in each region | `revenue_by_month_region` |
| Orders vs Invoices | Transaction counts and revenue for each event type, displayed on a split axis | `revenue_by_month_region` |

### What the figures show

- **Europe generates the most revenue**, followed by North America. Asia-Pacific and Latin America contribute considerably smaller amounts.
- **Revenue remains relatively steady from mid-2024 onwards.** April 2024 and October 2025 are partial months, which explains the lower figures at the beginning and end of the trend.
- **Orders account for roughly 70% of transactions and a similar share of revenue.** Invoices make up the remainder.

The distinction between orders and invoices is important when reading the figures. Both are retained as revenue-generating events in the model, so the headline totals include both. The comparison chart makes their respective contributions visible.

## 3. Section 2: Products & Refunds

This section looks at product performance alongside refund activity. It helps identify the strongest product categories and gives some context around how frequently transactions are refunded and why.

| Card | What it shows | Source table |
|---|---|---|
| Top 10 Products by Revenue | The ten highest-performing products by revenue | `product_performance` |
| Revenue by Category | Revenue generated by each product category | `product_performance` |
| Refund Rate by Category | Share of transactions refunded in each category | `refund_summary` |
| Refund Reasons | Refund counts and associated revenue, grouped by reason | `refund_reasons` |

### What the figures show

- **Developer Tools is the largest category by revenue**, followed by Design and AI Tools.
- **Refund rates are relatively low and fall within a narrow range**, approximately 1.3% to 2.3% across categories. The differences between categories are therefore fairly small.
- **Refund reasons are distributed fairly evenly** across billing errors, accidental purchases, duplicate orders and service dissatisfaction.

### How the category refund rate is calculated

The `refund_summary` table stores refund rates at the product level. However, the category chart does not simply average those individual rates.

Instead, it adds together the refunded and total transaction counts for all products in a category, then calculates the rate from those combined figures:

```sql
select
    category,
    round(
        100.0 * sum(refunded_transactions) / sum(transactions),
        2
    ) as refund_rate_pct
from marts.refund_summary
group by category
order by refund_rate_pct desc;
```

This approach gives each transaction equal weight in the category-level calculation. Simply averaging the product-level rates would give a product with three transactions the same influence as one with three thousand, which could distort the result.

## 4. Section 3: Customers & Commercial

The final section looks at purchasing behaviour across customer groups, sales channels, payment methods and discount codes. It brings together the customer and commercial metrics needed to understand how different parts of the business contribute to revenue.

| Card | What it shows | Source table |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue by Channel and Payment Method | Revenue for each sales channel and payment method combination | `channel_payment_summary` |
| Revenue by Segment | Revenue generated by each customer segment | `customer_segments` |
| Revenue per Customer | Average revenue per customer, grouped by segment | `customer_segments` |
| Revenue per Customer by Age Band | Average revenue per customer across age groups | `customer_segments` |
| Discount Impact | Transaction counts, basket size and revenue for each discount code | `discount_impact` |

### What the figures show

- **Consumers account for most of the revenue**, largely because there are considerably more customers in this segment, approximately 2,500. Their higher total contribution does not mean that each consumer necessarily spends more.
- **Revenue per customer is fairly consistent across segments**, ranging from roughly $7.7k to $8.0k. The figures are also similar across age bands, at approximately $7.6k to $8.1k.
- **Most transactions do not use a discount code.** The discount analysis makes it possible to compare typical basket values and revenue between discounted transactions and those without a discount.

### How revenue per customer is calculated

As with the category refund rate, revenue per customer is calculated from the underlying totals rather than by averaging ratios from smaller groups.

For example, to calculate revenue per customer by segment:

```sql
select
    segment,
    sum(customers) as customers,
    round(
        sum(revenue_usd_incl_tax) / sum(customers),
        0
    ) as revenue_per_customer_usd
from marts.customer_segments
group by segment;
```

Summing the customer counts across the age-band and acquisition-channel groups is safe here because each customer belongs to exactly one combination of segment, age band and acquisition channel.

This produces a properly weighted result rather than treating every subgroup as equally sized.

## 5. Reading the numbers correctly

A few details about the underlying data and calculations are worth keeping in mind when interpreting the dashboards:

- **Revenue includes tax by default.** Unless a chart explicitly says otherwise, the revenue figures use `revenue_usd_incl_tax`. The model also provides `revenue_usd_ex_tax` for analysis that excludes tax.
- **Orders and invoices both contribute to revenue.** Headline totals include both event types, while the Orders vs Invoices chart separates their contributions.
- **Refund rate measures transactions, not revenue.** It represents the proportion of transactions that were refunded, rather than the proportion of revenue lost to refunds.
- **The first and last months are partial.** April 2024 and October 2025 do not cover full months, so their lower figures should not be interpreted as a sudden drop in sales.
- **The figures reflect the last verified run.** They are observations from the current source dataset, not fixed values. Processing a different workbook can change the results.

## 6. Design choices

A few decisions shaped how the dashboards are organised and how the charts present the data.

**One chart, one main idea.** Early versions combined transaction counts, revenue and rates on dual axes. This made smaller series difficult to read, particularly when the measures operated on very different scales. The final dashboards separate those comparisons where necessary or use tables when they communicate the information more clearly.

**Calculate ratios from their underlying totals.** Refund rates and revenue-per-customer figures are calculated from summed counts and revenue rather than by averaging ratios from individual groups. This avoids giving smaller groups disproportionate influence over the overall result.

**Keep reporting access read-only.** Metabase connects through `bi_reader`, which has access to the `marts` schema only. This keeps dashboard users within the reporting layer and prevents them from accessing or modifying the underlying raw and staging data.

## 7. Possible improvements

There are a few areas where the dashboards could be extended:

- **Group product versions into families.** Use `base_key` in `dim_products` to compare performance across different versions of the same product.
- **Improve channel comparisons.** A stacked bar chart could make the contribution of each payment method within a sales channel easier to compare.
- **Add shared filters.** Date-range and region filters across all three sections would make it easier to explore specific periods and markets.
- **Move Metabase into the VPC.** Hosting it within the AWS environment would make the dashboards available beyond a local machine and provide a better foundation for shared access. See the hardening notes in [03 · Infrastructure](03-infrastructure.md).
